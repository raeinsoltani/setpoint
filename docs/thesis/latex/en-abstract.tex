\en-abstract{
Horizontal autoscaling adjusts the number of replicas of a service in proportion to
incoming load. Kubernetes' default mechanism, the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler, has two
well-known limitations: it relies mainly on CPU utilisation, which is an indirect proxy
for real application load, and it is purely reactive, acting only after load has already
risen. The conventional remedy for the second limitation is to add forecasting to the
decision loop.

This project designed and implemented a custom autoscaler in Go that reads its scaling
signal from Prometheus through a configurable PromQL expression, takes its policy from a
configuration file, and adjusts the replica count through the Kubernetes API. Three
policies were implemented: threshold-based, predictive on total load, and predictive on
per-replica load.

The project's principal finding, however, emerged during evaluation and concerns not the
autoscaler itself but closed-loop stability. When the forecast is applied to per-replica
load, the forecaster extrapolates a signal that the controller's own actions move, and
the loop acquires positive gain. This instability is almost invisible in conventional
quality-of-service and cost metrics, because the stabilization mechanism masks it. With
that mechanism removed, the same policy reverses direction 119 times over the 120 control
intervals of an 1800-second run and reaches 50.8\% SLA violations, whereas with a
90-second stabilization window it passes every test in the suite.

The evaluation was carried out on a real Kubernetes cluster across four workload patterns
and nine comparison arms, comprising 49 valid runs in total. The correct predictive
policy achieves the lowest SLA violation rate and the lowest under-provisioning on all
four workloads, and its median reaction time on the diurnal workload is roughly four
times better than that of the default autoscaler driven by the same custom metric; but
this advantage is bought with roughly 6 to 20 percent more capacity. The defensible claim is
therefore a trade-off rather than outright dominance. The methodological conclusion is
that evaluating the stability of a predictive policy requires disabling the stabilization
mechanism and using a workload with discontinuities; otherwise the evaluation measures
something that has already been hidden.
}
